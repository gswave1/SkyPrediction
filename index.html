How the number is built

Two things have to line up. Horizon asks whether direct light can even get through: the model samples the sky at 30, 80, 150, 250 km out along the sun's true azimuth for that spot and date, and counts low and mid cloud sitting in that corridor. The light that paints cloud overhead has to travel under the deck from 100–300 km away, so a solid wall anywhere in that window shuts the show down even when the sky right above you is perfect; the 150 km cell counts most, the 30 km cell least. The card shows how much of each cell is blocked. Canvas asks whether there's anything overhead worth lighting — high cloud scores best around 55% cover, mid cloud around 45%, and a low deck above you knocks it down hard. Clarity folds in precipitation, visibility and humidity at showtime, plus aerosols from the CAMS air-quality forecast: haze and wildfire smoke don't add colour, they mute it, so aerosol optical depth above about 0.15 starts cutting the score and 0.6+ (smoke) takes up to half of it. Rain that fell 3–18 hours before showtime and has since cleared earns a bonus — post-front air is the cleanest you get. Aerosol data runs about five days out; past that, clarity scores without it. View fit checks whether the sun's azimuth falls inside the arc that spot is actually open to, so a west-facing dock doesn't get credit for a sunrise.

score = (0.88·canvas + 0.12·view) × (0.5 + 0.5·clarity) × (0.12 + 0.88·horizon) — sky potential, dimmed by dirty air, multiplied by whether the light gets delivered. Each spot is scored on the hour the sun crosses and the adjacent hour on the golden side, blended.

Cirrus, models and fog

Cirrus. Every model reports high cloud, but only ECMWF and Météo-France actually populate cloud at the 300, 250 and 200 hPa pressure levels — the cirrus band itself. The app pulls those three levels plus humidity at 300 hPa from ECMWF and treats them as evidence about how much high cloud is actually up there, not as a nudge. When the profile confirms cirrus — 25% or more in the band with humid air at 300 hPa — the score uses 80% of that reading in place of the scoring model's own high-cloud figure if that figure is lower. When a model claims a lot of high cloud but the band is nearly empty and dry, the deck is almost certainly altostratus sitting lower than the model labels it, and the figure is cut back. The card shows the substitution as 6→26 so you can always see when it happened and by how much.

Model agreement. Consensus scores every spot against three models — HRDPS, HRRR/GFS and ECMWF — and takes a weighted average rather than a flat one. Those three are also the only ones offered: HRDPS resolves this terrain at 2.5 km, HRRR at 3 km inside a day, and ECMWF carries the medium range. ICON and GEM sat at the bottom of the weighting and GEM partly duplicates HRDPS; UKMO's high-resolution grid covers only the UK, so over New England it was a delayed 10 km global. Picking any of the three is free, because the data is already in hand. The weights shift with lead time, because the models don't rank the same at every range. Inside 24 hours the convection-allowing HRRR run leads (2.2) with HRDPS behind it (1.5) — that's the same-day planning case. One to two days out HRDPS at 2.5 km takes over (1.9) and HRRR drops to 1.4. Past 48 hours both high-resolution runs have ended, GFS and HRDPS fall back to 1.0, and ECMWF carries the medium range at 1.6. The line under the controls names which band you're in and which model is leading it. The spread between all contributing models is shown beside the score, because an 80 every model sees is a different proposition from an 80 only one of them sees. Pick a single model from the dropdown to score against it alone.

Forecast horizons. HRDPS stops at about 48 hours and HRRR at a similar range, after which the board falls back to the models that run further out. A model past its horizon is dropped from the average entirely — never counted as clear sky — and the card names which ones dropped out, so a day-five number is visibly built on fewer, coarser opinions than a day-one number.

Map imagery. All four views — Map, Terrain, Satellite and Hybrid — come from Google Maps, using the Google key set under Key. The same key fills in drive times in the spot editor. Terrain is the one worth having: it shows the ridge a spot sits behind and how much tree cover stands between the pullout and the horizon, which is exactly what the open-arc guesses keep getting wrong.

The map. Same scores, placed geographically — each spot is a dot coloured by its score for the day and event you're looking at, sized by how good it is. It answers the question the ranked list can't: if one part of the state looks promising, what else is near it. Click a dot and the popup lists every other spot within 25 km with its own score, so you can plan a sunset and the blue hour after it, or a sunrise and a fog stop, without driving back out.

Finding a spot. Type into the Spot box to filter the board down to matching names and towns — "notch", "lake", "kanc" — and the ranked list narrows as you type. Pick one of the suggestions (or press Enter to take the first) and it opens that spot on its own.

One spot at a time. Choose a spot from the Spot box (or click its name on any card) and the board switches to a single-location view: every day in the forecast, sunrise and sunset side by side with lake fog, and the best window called out with its full breakdown. That's the view for planning a specific hike on a specific day rather than deciding where to go tonight.

Lake fog. Scored over the four hours ending at sunrise: clear sky overnight for radiational cooling, wind under about 6 km/h, and a dew point sitting on top of the air temperature. Water spots get a steam-fog term from the drop between the previous afternoon's high and the temperature at first light — the bigger the drop over water that's still warm, the more it steams.

Staying inside the allowance. Open-Meteo bills a request as locations × variables × models, and a free account gets 600 of those per minute. The app keeps its own ledger of what it has spent and holds off rather than triggering a refusal: cloud comes from three models in one nine-series request, everything else — visibility, precipitation, humidity, temperature, dew point, wind and the three cirrus levels — rides in a single ten-series ECMWF request, and probe points within 20 km of each other share one fetch. A default refresh costs about 200 calls instead of 580. Opening the drive filter all the way is still more than a minute's allowance, so it will ask you to narrow it.

Live data from Open-Meteo, cached for two hours. Anything past about 48 hours moves a lot — treat the far days as a planning hint, not a promise. Your spot list lives in this browser; use Export in the spot editor to keep a copy.

The open arc is the compass range this spot actually has a clear view across — measured clockwise from the "from" bearing to the "to" bearing. A dock looking west across water might be 175° to 330°. A bald summit is 0° to 360°. Sunsets here run about 240°–300° through the year; sunrises about 60°–120°.
